    What the research says

    Dr. Zakaria explains this part in about two minutes.

    Grolnick calls what Mia does praising the process. A description tells a child what she did, so she knows what to do again tomorrow. "Good job" tells her only that the teacher is pleased. In the study Grolnick describes, fifth graders who heard "you must be really smart" chose easier tasks afterward, and after a hard task they enjoyed the work less and blamed their ability. Children who heard "you must have worked hard" did the opposite. Motivation Myth Busters, pages 241 to 242.

    The guide puts Mia's narration under Recommendation 1 as well as Recommendation 3. A teacher who names what a child did is also showing the rest of the class what the expectation looks like. That is why the guide's praise sentences carry a name and a behavior. Summary page 4 and page 8.

    There is a catch, and your second answer probably found it. Grolnick notes on page 242 that praise by itself can be a problem. The guide's panel says on page 37 that some children feel singled out by public praise. A teacher asks each child whether a quiet word or a note works better, and watches how the child reacts.

    Question 3

    Twice in this clip Mia says, "Don't say it. Don't say it. She's still working." What is Mia protecting when she stops a child from blurting the answer? Is this a precorrection or a correction? Say why.

    Saved. It is a correction, because it comes after the child starts to speak. A precorrection would come before the number talk, in words like "When a friend is still thinking, we keep the answer in our heads." The guide says a reminder comes before the moment a child usually struggles. Summary page 5.
